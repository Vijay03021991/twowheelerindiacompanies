Income Statement

Revenue also called Sales, Turnover, Top Line

Revenue is the total amount of money a business brings in from selling its goods or services. It's often called the "top line" because it sits at the very top of the income statement — and it excludes any tax collected on behalf of the government, such as CGST, SGST or IGST.

Revenue can be reported as both standalone and consolidated if a company owns other businesses. The choice between the two depends entirely on whether you're looking at the core parent business by itself, or the entire corporate group as a single entity.

Standalone Revenue

Reflects the money generated solely by the parent company as an independent legal entity. It completely excludes the earnings of any smaller companies it owns.

Example. If a major tech parent company generates ₹10,000 crores from its primary digital services, its standalone revenue is exactly ₹10,000 crores.

When to use it: to evaluate how the main, core business is performing on its own legs, without help from its child companies.

Consolidated Revenue

Shows the combined total money brought in by the parent company plus all of its subsidiaries, joint ventures, and associate businesses.

The catch — intercompany eliminations. If the parent company sells ₹1,000 crores of goods to its own subsidiary, that inner-group transaction is deleted from the consolidated statement. This prevents the group from artificially inflating its sales figures.
Example. If the tech parent generates ₹10,000 crores and its retail subsidiary brings in ₹5,000 crores from external customers, consolidated revenue is ₹15,000 crores.

When to use it: when evaluating a stock — it gives the true, complete financial picture of the entire corporate group.

Common misconception

Why Consolidated Revenue Isn't a Simple Sum

You might expect Consolidated Revenue to be every sale from every company a parent has invested in, added together. It isn't — accounting rules strictly forbid that unless the parent has total control. Here's why a parent can own a huge chunk of a successful startup, yet show ₹0 of that startup's sales in its Consolidated Revenue.

1. The rule of control — over 50% vs. under 50%. The accounting world splits investments into two categories, based strictly on who runs the show.

Subsidiary > 50% owned

The parent owns more than half the business, so it has full control and calls all the shots.

Rule. 100% of the subsidiary's revenue and expenses get added directly into the parent's Consolidated Revenue, line by line.

Associate 20–50% owned

The parent holds a large stake — say, a 34% stake in a company like Euler Motors — but not control. This counts as "significant influence" only.

Rule. The associate is never line-by-line consolidated — see below.

2. The "one-line" rule for associates. For an associate, accountants use the Equity Method. Under it, the parent is completely banned from touching the associate's top-line revenue — even if that associate makes thousands of crores in sales, not a single rupee is added to the parent's Consolidated Revenue. Instead, the parent looks only at the associate's very last number — its Net Profit or Net Loss — works out its exact ownership share of that number, and drops it onto a single line at the bottom of its own report, labeled "Share of Profit/Loss of Associates."

3. How this plays out in the numbers. Many high-growth tech and EV companies spend heavily on factories and R&D, so they often run at a net loss in their early years. Because of the two rules above, that shows up in the parent's books like this:

  • Zero revenue boost — the parent's Consolidated Revenue looks small, because the associate's massive sales figures are left out completely
  • A hit to profits — if the associate loses money, the parent must subtract its ownership share of that loss (for example, ₹107.79 crores) directly from its own Consolidated Net Profit
Summary. If a parent doesn't own more than half (>50%) of a business, it can't claim that business's sales as its own. It can only reflect its share of the final net profit or loss, as a single line at the very bottom of the page.

Bill of materials

Cost of Goods Sold The direct cost of building what was sold — not head office, not marketing, just the factory.

The seven components of COGS

Shown as equal spokes on purpose — no weighting yet. Once real cost-of-materials figures come in, this can be redrawn to scale.

Direct cost · 01 of 07

Direct Materials

The physical stuff bolted, glued, or poured into the vehicle. If it becomes a permanent part of the product, it's counted here.

~62% of total COGS

Core idea. Direct materials are usually the single largest cost a manufacturer carries, and the one most exposed to forces it can't control — steel and aluminum prices move with global commodity markets, not with how efficiently the factory is run.

Most readers assume "materials" means only raw steel and plastic. It also includes ready-made parts bought from outside suppliers — tires, batteries, brake calipers — anything that arrives finished and gets bolted straight onto the product.

Key characteristics:

  • Commodity-exposed — steel, aluminum and rubber prices swing with global markets, not company performance
  • Includes bought-out parts — tires, batteries, brake calipers, instrument clusters from outside vendors
  • Consumables count too — engine oil, brake fluid, paint sprayed on at the factory
  • Packaging included — protective film and crates used before the product ships out
  • Usually the biggest single COGS line for any vehicle manufacturer
Direct cost · 02 of 07

Direct Labor

Wages paid to the people whose physical hands or machine operation turn raw materials into a finished product — not the people who manage them.

~9% of total COGS

Core idea. Direct labor is deliberately narrow. It only counts pay for workers directly transforming the product — assembly, welding, painting. The moment someone's job is to supervise, plan, or support rather than build, their salary moves out of COGS and into Operating Expenses instead.

A common mix-up: production bonuses count here because they're tied to output, but a plant manager's salary does not — even though both people work inside the same factory.

Key characteristics:

  • Assembly line wages — workers fitting engines, wiring, wheels
  • Welding & paint shop pay — specialized technicians on chassis and spray booths
  • Output-linked bonuses — extra pay tied to meeting daily production targets
  • Contract & temporary labor — workers hired for surge capacity on the line
  • Excludes supervisors and plant management — that's Fixed Overhead, not Direct Labor
Direct cost · 03 of 07

Variable Manufacturing Overhead

Factory costs that rise and fall automatically with how much the factory produces. Slow the line down, and these costs shrink with it.

~8% of total COGS

Core idea. This is the clearest test of "variable" in accounting: if production stopped completely for a week, this entire category would fall close to zero. That's what separates it from Fixed Overhead, which keeps running whether or not a single product is built.

Job-work payments to outside vendors — like sending a batch of gears out for heat treatment — belong here too, even though the work happens outside the company's own factory walls.

Key characteristics:

  • Scales with output — rises and falls directly with production volume
  • Machine power — electricity for welding robots, stamping presses, paint ovens
  • Tooling wear — drill bits and cutting tools replaced after set production cycles
  • Outsourced job-work — vendors handling specific processing steps on batches
  • Quality testing — materials consumed during emission and calibration checks
Direct cost · 04 of 07

Fixed Manufacturing Overhead

The baseline cost of simply keeping the factory open — whether it builds 1 product or 10,000 this month.

~6% of total COGS

Core idea. Fixed overhead doesn't care about volume. Rent is rent whether the line runs at full capacity or sits idle for maintenance — which is exactly why it's dangerous during a slowdown. Revenue falls with fewer units sold, but this cost stays the same, squeezing margins.

One deliberate exclusion here: depreciation on factory machinery is not included in this bucket. It's tracked as its own separate line under Operating Expenses, so it isn't counted twice across the income statement.

Key characteristics:

  • Doesn't scale with volume — same cost whether output is high or low
  • Factory rent & lease — for the land and buildings production happens in
  • Plant insurance — coverage against fire, flood, industrial accidents
  • Factory management pay — supervisors, safety officers, plant managers, security
  • Depreciation is excluded — reported separately under Operating Expenses
Direct cost · 05 of 07

Logistics Tied to Production

The cost of getting raw materials and components from suppliers to the factory gate — inbound only.

~5% of total COGS

Core idea. The direction matters here. Freight-in — shipping steel, batteries, or imported components into the factory — is a production cost. Freight-out — shipping a finished product to a dealer — is a completely different cost, classified as a selling expense, not COGS.

Import duties on components sourced from overseas suppliers, like electronic microchips or fuel-injector parts, count in this bucket too, alongside the shipping cost itself.

Key characteristics:

  • Inbound only — materials and parts coming in, not finished goods going out
  • Ocean freight & customs — shipping fees and import tariffs on overseas components
  • Domestic trucking — hauling steel or batteries from suppliers to the factory
  • Excludes freight-out — shipping finished products to dealers is a selling expense instead
Direct cost · 06 of 07

Change in Inventory

Not a real cash cost — an accounting adjustment that lines up spending with what was actually sold.

~6% of total COGS

Core idea. A factory doesn't finish and sell every product in the same month it starts building it. This line makes sure the cost of half-built or unsold products doesn't hit the income statement early — it only counts as a cost once a dealer actually buys the finished vehicle.

This is the one COGS component that can be genuinely confusing at first: an increase in inventory actually reduces reported COGS for the period, because that cost hasn't "left" the company yet — it's just sitting on the balance sheet as stock.

Key characteristics:

  • Timing adjustment, not new spending — reallocates cost to when a sale actually happens
  • Work-in-progress — half-assembled products still on the line at period-end
  • Finished goods in storage — completed products waiting in a warehouse, unsold
  • Rising inventory lowers COGS — cost sits on the balance sheet until the product sells
Direct cost · 07 of 07

Write-Downs, Scrap & Obsolescence

The cost of material that entered the factory but never became a product anyone could sell.

~4% of total COGS

Core idea. Every factory loses some material to waste — that's normal. What matters for an investor is the trend. A rising write-down line over several years can quietly signal declining quality control, aging equipment, or products falling out of date faster than the company can sell through old parts.

A mid-year design change can turn perfectly good inventory into waste overnight — A company switching from carburetors to fuel injection, for example, would have made any unused carburetor stock instantly obsolete.

Key characteristics:

  • Production scrap — leftover raw material waste, like stamped-out steel offcuts
  • In-process damage — parts dropped, scratched, or broken during assembly
  • Engineering obsolescence — components made unusable by a mid-year design change
  • Worth watching over time — a rising trend can flag quality or planning problems
01 – 07 sum to Total COGS
Revenue − COGS = Gross Profit

Gross Profit

What's left after paying only the direct cost of building the products that were sold — before a single rupee of marketing, R&D, admin salaries, depreciation, interest, or tax is counted.

Tracked side by side, Revenue and COGS reveal how much came in and what it directly cost to build, for every single year — the first, cleanest signal of whether the business is getting more or less efficient at making the product over time.
